Frequencies of C major, Part 2: One Octave as a Ring

November 2026

Part 2 of 7 in Frequencies of C major.

Previous: Part 1: Keys and Hertz | Next: Part 3: Harmonics: Why Half a Turn and a Quarter Turn

Part 1 put one octave, from middle C to the C above, on a frequency axis. Halfway from C to the next C in hertz is ×1½, and with the real keys it falls at the seventh key, G, not at the sixth, F♯.

This part bends that octave into a ring, so that its two Cs meet at the top. Halfway along the octave becomes half a turn round the ring, directly across from C.

1. Roll the octave into a ring

The picture below starts with the octave as a straight line, its keys spaced evenly, the way they sit on a keyboard. Drag Roll to bend the line into a ring. The line doesn't stretch. It only bends, until its two ends meet at the top. Both ends are C, so C sits at the top of the ring.

Spaced like the keys, every key gets a twelfth of a turn, 30°. The sixth key, F♯, lands exactly halfway round, directly across from C. The dashed line marks halfway.

Now drag Spacing from keys to hertz. The notes slide round the ring. Nothing about them changes: each one still vibrates at the same frequency, and tapping it still plays the same sound. Only where they're drawn changes.

Watch the ×1½ mark. Spaced like the keys, it sits at 210.6°, just past G. Spaced by hertz, each note's place is how far its frequency has climbed from C towards the next C. The next C vibrates twice as fast, 261.6 Hz faster, so ×1½, at 392.4 Hz, is halfway there: half a turn. The mark slides round to the half turn and carries G with it. G ends up 0.6° short of the half turn, and F♯ slides back to 149.1°.

The other marks follow the same rule. ×1¼ is a quarter of the climb, so it ends up a quarter turn round, and E lands 3.6° past it.

Spaced by hertz and rolled up, this is the frequency ring. A note's angle on it is 360° × (its frequency ÷ C's frequency − 1), so ×1½ is half a turn and ×1¼ a quarter turn. On the frequency ring, the note across from C is G.

Musicians: spaced like the keys, the ring is the chromatic circle, with the tritone, F♯, opposite C. The frequency ring is neither that nor the circle of fifths: it places each note by its frequency. Its half turn is 3:2, the perfect fifth. The equal-tempered fifth is about 2 cents narrower than 3:2, and on the frequency ring that's the 0.6° gap.

2. Seven keys and five

The readout under the ring counts the keys in each half, and the hertz each half covers.

Spaced like the keys, each half holds six keys, but the halves don't cover the same hertz. From C up to halfway, F♯ at 370.0 Hz, the first half covers 108.4 Hz. From F♯ on to the next C, the second half covers 153.3 Hz, 41% more.

Spaced by hertz, each half covers the same 130.8 Hz. Now the keys are uneven: seven climb the first half, C♯ to G, and five climb the second, A♭ to the next C.

Something has to be uneven. Space the keys evenly and the halves cover unequal hertz. Space the hertz evenly and the halves hold unequal numbers of keys. On a keyboard, the keys are evenly spaced, so halfway looks like the sixth key, F♯. In hertz, halfway is G, seven keys up, with five keys left above it.

Musicians: seven keys and five are the perfect fifth, C to G, and the perfect fourth, G to C. Going up from C, the fifth and then the fourth cover the same number of hertz, because ×1½ is halfway from ×1 to ×2.

3. The same ring for every octave

Move Spacing all the way to hertz and press Add the next octave. Every note in the octave above, C5 to C6, vibrates twice as fast as the note of the same name below it, so that octave covers twice as many hertz: 523.3 Hz. Spaced by hertz, it's twice as long, and it rolls into a ring twice the size, around the same centre.

Each of its notes lands at the same angle as the note of the same name on the inner ring. Every octave is the same twelve multiplications, so the angles on one ring hold for every octave. Drag Roll back to a line and the two octaves sit end to end, the second twice as long as the first, as they did on Part 1's frequency axis. Tap a note on either ring to hear it.

4. Where the angle comes from

There's another way to find a note's place on the frequency ring, using time instead of hertz.

Picture each note as a hand on a dial, turning once for every vibration. Both hands start at the top. Drag Time until C's hand has turned once. G vibrates 1½ times as fast, so by then G's hand has turned 1½ times, and it points halfway round: the half turn. Where a note's hand points after one turn of C is its place on the frequency ring.

Keep dragging. After two turns of C, G has turned exactly three times, and both hands are back at the top together. Choose E or F to see the same thing take longer. E, at exactly ×1¼, meets C at the top after 4 turns of C and 5 of E. F, at exactly ×1⅓, meets C after 3 turns of C and 4 of F.

Now choose G again and switch Tuning to Piano. The piano's G vibrates a little under 1½ times as fast as C. After one turn of C, its hand points at 179.4°, the same angle G has on the frequency ring. After two turns of C, it misses the top by 1.2°, so the hands aren't back together. Slightly off, 0.6% below the exact ratio, misses by 6.5°.

5. Listen

Press Listen together to hear C and the second note at once. With the exact ratio, the sound is steady. Slightly off, it wavers, about five times a second for G. The piano's G wavers too, but slowly, a little under once a second. Part 3 shows where the wavering comes from, and why an exact ×1½ makes it stop.

Musicians: the wavering is beating. For C and G, the beat comes from C's third harmonic against G's second, and the equal-tempered fifth beats a little under once a second at middle C.

What comes next

Spaced by hertz and rolled into a ring, one octave puts ×1½ half a turn from C and ×1¼ a quarter turn. G sits 0.6° short of the half turn and E 3.6° past the quarter turn. The ring's two halves split the keys seven and five.

Part 3 asks why the half turn and the quarter turn matter. Every note contains harmonics, and on the frequency ring they land on the halves and quarters.
