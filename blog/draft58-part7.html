Frequencies of C major, Part 7: Pythagoras and Fixed Keys

November 2026

Part 7 of 7 in Frequencies of C major.

Previous: Part 6: Eight out of Twelve | Next: none

Part 6 built C major from C, its two partners, and the notes beside the quarter and half turns of their rings. It leaned on the piano's keys twice. On a piano, a partner is always exactly seven keys up, and four hops along the chain land exactly on the quarter-turn note.

With exact ratios, every partner exactly ×1½ and every quarter-turn note exactly ×1¼, neither quite holds. This part measures by how much, and then looks at what an instrument with fixed keys can do about it.

1. The pure answer

The tradition credits Pythagoras, about 2,500 years ago, with the first recipe: build the notes from exact hops of ×1½. Inside one octave, it makes complete sense. G at exactly ×1½ sits exactly on the half turn of C's ring, its harmonic 2 lands exactly on C's harmonic 3, and the two hold steady (Part 3). Every hop along the chain is as steady as that one.

The trouble comes when the hops spill over into the next octaves. Part 5's chain needs twelve hops to get round, and twelve hops climb seven octaves.

2. Measuring the misses

The ring below is middle C's frequency ring, with the piano's keys as dots. Each route is drawn hop by hop, every landing halved back into the octave from middle C, with its miss in red.

Twelve partner hops. Twelve hops of exactly ×1½ multiply to 129.75, but seven octaves multiply to 128. So the twelfth hop doesn't land on C: it lands 4.9° past the top of the ring, 3.6 Hz above middle C. The chain of partners doesn't close.

Four hops and a quarter turn. Part 5 found two routes to E: four partner hops, or one quarter turn. With exact ratios, four hops reach ×1.2656 (81/64) and the quarter turn reaches ×1¼ (5/4). The two Es land 5.6° apart: 4.1 Hz at middle C.

Three quarter turns. On a piano, three hops of four keys make an octave. Three exact quarter turns of ×1¼ multiply to 1.953, not 2, so they land 16.9° short of the top, at 511.0 Hz: 12.3 Hz below C5, the C above middle C.

Now drag Tuning towards the piano's keys. Every hop changes by the same tiny factor, and every miss shrinks to nothing. Twelve hops of seven keys are exactly seven octaves, four hops of seven keys land on the same key as one hop of four, and three hops of four keys are exactly one octave.

Musicians: the twelve-hop miss is the Pythagorean comma, about 23.5 cents. The gap between the two Es is the syntonic comma, 81/80, about 21.5 cents. The shortfall of three thirds is the lesser diesis, about 41 cents. In exact tuning, enharmonic spellings such as B♯ and C name different frequencies.

3. Fixed keys

A singer or a violinist can nudge a note as they go, a little higher here and a little lower there. A keyboard can't. Each key plays one frequency, in every piece, and there are twelve keys to the octave. The misses have to go somewhere. The picture below compares three ways of fixing the keys, by how fast four pairs of notes waver. Tap a pair to zoom in on its matching harmonics and hear it.

Pythagoras's keys. Tune the keys by eleven exact hops up from C: G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯ and, last, the key for F, reached as E♯. Every one of those links is exact, so C and G hold steady, and so do D and A. But the twelfth link, from that F back up to C, has to take the whole miss: C sits 7.3° short of the half turn of F's ring. Played from the F below middle C, the pair wavers about seven times a second. Its old name is the wolf. And E, four exact hops up from C, wavers against C about 16 times a second.

Exact triangles. Tune Part 6's three triangles exactly instead: E at ×1¼, G at ×1½, F at ×1⅓, A at ×1⅔, B at ×1⅞, and D, as G's partner, at ×1⅛ (9/8). Now C and G hold steady, and so do F and C, and C and E. But look at D and A. A is D's partner, yet here A is only ×1.4815 (40/27) of D: 6.7° short of the half turn of D's ring, and the pair wavers about eleven times a second. For D and A to be exact partners, D would have to be ×1⅑ (10/9), 3.6 Hz lower. One key can't be both. It would need a switch: 9/8 alongside G, 10/9 alongside A.

The piano's keys. The piano shares every miss out evenly. Every key is the same multiplication, ×1.0595, so every hop of seven keys is ×1.4983, a hair under ×1½, and twelve of them make exactly seven octaves. Every partner sits 0.6° short of the half turn of its ring, and every quarter-turn note 3.6° past the quarter turn. Nothing is exact, so every pair wavers a little: the partner pairs a little under once a second around middle C, and C and E about ten times. But the misses are the same in every key, no link takes the whole miss of the twelve hops, and no key needs a switch.

Musicians: Pythagoras's keys are Pythagorean tuning, with its wolf fifth. Exact triangles are just intonation, where the two Ds differ by the syntonic comma. The piano's keys are equal temperament: every fifth about 2 cents narrow and every major third about 14 cents wide. Between them in history came meantone and well temperaments, which shared the misses out unevenly, favouring the common keys.

4. What the piano's answer pays for

The piano's keys bought something this series has used all along. Because every key is the same multiplication, every root's ring has the same places. That's what let Part 4 move the root anywhere and find the same pattern, what made Part 5's chain close after twelve hops, and what gave every note in Part 6 a triangle of the same shape. On Pythagoras's keys or on exact triangles, the rings aren't all alike: on Pythagoras's keys, F's ring is the one with the wolf.

The price is that no partner sits exactly on the half turn, and no quarter-turn note exactly on the quarter turn. G's 0.6° from Part 2, and E's 3.6°, are that price, paid in every key.

The whole series, in one picture

  1. Every key multiplies the frequency by the same amount, so evenly spaced keys are growing leaps in hertz. (Part 1)
  2. Rolled up by hertz, one octave makes the frequency ring. Halfway round is ×1½, beside G, and the two halves split the keys seven and five. (Part 2)
  3. Harmonics are evenly spaced in hertz, so they share the ring out in halves, quarters and eighths. The half turn is harmonic 3's spot, and the quarter turn harmonic 5's. (Part 3)
  4. Moving the root up five keys, to F, brings C to the half turn, because of the split into seven and five. (Part 4)
  5. Partners, a half turn apart, line all twelve notes up in one chain. (Part 5)
  6. C and its two partners, with the notes beside their quarter and half turns, give the seven notes of C major. (Part 6)
  7. Exact ratios don't close, so fixed keys have to put the misses somewhere, and the piano shares them out evenly. (Part 7)

The main picture in each of Parts 1 to 6 could slide between evenly spaced keys and hertz, with its rule printed underneath. Spaced like the keys, the same rules put F♯ across from C, needed six moves where five do, broke the chain of partners into two notes, and gave four notes instead of C major. Spaced by hertz, they found G, F, the chain and the seven notes.
