The Frequency Ring, Part 2: One Octave, Rolled into a Ring

November 2026

Part 2 of 7 in The Frequency Ring.

Previous: Part 1: Stickers and Elastic | Next: Part 3: Harmonics: Why the Ring Works

Part 1 ended with two rulers that disagree. On the carpenter's keyboard, every key is the same width. On the tuner's chart, every hertz is the same width, so the keys spread out as you go up, each gap about 6% wider than the last.

This part cuts one octave out of each ruler, from middle C to the C above it, and rolls it up so the two Cs meet. The two rulers make two very different rings. One of them, the tuner's, is the ring this series is named after.

1. Two ways to roll an octave

Start with the Chart band: one octave of the tuner's chart, with its keys unevenly spaced. Drag Roll until the ends meet. Then try the Keyboard band and roll that. Tap any note to hear it.

The keyboard band rolls into a familiar picture: twelve notes evenly spaced like the hours on a clock, each one a twelfth of a turn from the next.

The chart band is different. Its notes crowd together just after C and spread out as they go round. The seam at the top is easy to spot: the widest gap, B to C, sits right next to the narrowest, C to C♯. The ticks on this ring are multipliers rather than hours. A note's place on it is its frequency's share of the way from C to the next C. A note that vibrates ×1¼ as fast as C sits a quarter turn round, and a note at ×1½ sits half a turn round.

Tap E and G. E lands just past the quarter turn, and G lands just short of half a turn, almost exactly opposite C. On the keyboard band, G was only 7/12 of the way round. On the chart band, G sits across from C, as far from it as any note on the ring can be. This series calls the chart band's ring the frequency ring.

Musicians: the keyboard band rolls into the chromatic circle. The frequency ring is a different circle, and it's not the circle of fifths either. G sits opposite C here because the ring is spaced by frequency rather than by key. Part 4 shows how the circle of fifths grows out of it.

Now turn on Add the next octave. The octave above is twice as long on the tuner's chart, so it rolls into a ring twice the size. Every note on the outer ring lands at exactly the same angle as its namesake on the inner ring. Each octave of the chart has the same pattern, just bigger.

2. What the angle means: clock hands

Why should ×1½ be half a turn? Here's a way to see it without any rolling.

Give each note a clock hand that turns once for every vibration. C's hand turns 261.6 times a second, and G's turns about one and a half times as fast. Slow everything down so you can watch, and drag Time.

Stop at one full turn of C. By then G's hand has turned one and a half times, so it points half a turn round, straight down. That's the frequency ring's rule: a note's angle is where its hand points when C's hand completes one turn. Try E: after one turn of C, E has turned 1¼ times, so it points a quarter turn round.

Keep going to two turns of C. G has now turned exactly three times, and both hands are back at the top together. In the time C vibrates twice, G vibrates three times. That's what ×1½ really means: not a decimal, but a count of two against three. Other notes have their own counts at their exact ratios. E meets C again after four turns of C (five of E), and F after three (four of F).

Now switch G's tuning to Slightly off, just 0.6% below ×1½. After two turns of C, G's hand misses the top. The hands never quite meet again.

3. Listen

Press Listen together with G on Exact ratio, then on Slightly off. The exact pair sounds smooth and steady. The slightly-off pair wavers, a pulsing about five times a second, even though G moved by less than a hundredth.

Then try Piano. The piano's G is very slightly below ×1½, and if you listen closely, the sound swells slowly, a little under once a second. It's not a mistake in the piano. It's a choice, and Part 6 explains it.

Keep that wavering in mind. It's the clue that exact counts like two against three matter, and Part 3 shows why.

Musicians: G against C is a perfect fifth, the 3:2 ratio. An equal-tempered fifth is about two cents narrow of 3:2, and the slow swell you hear is its beating. Part 7 comes back to beats and how tuners use them.

What comes next

On the frequency ring, a note's angle is its ratio to C. ×1½ is half a turn and ×1¼ is a quarter turn, and those simple fractions are also simple counts: two against three, four against five. Notes whose counts are exact sound steady, and notes that are slightly off waver.

But why should simple counts sound good at all? Part 3 answers that with harmonics: the extra, quieter notes hidden inside every note. Dropped onto the frequency ring, they fall into a pattern that explains where G and E land.
