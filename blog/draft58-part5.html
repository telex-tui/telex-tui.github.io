Frequencies of C major, Part 5: Following the Partners

November 2026

Part 5 of 7 in Frequencies of C major.

Previous: Part 4: Rotating the Root | Next: Part 6: Eight out of Twelve (coming)

Part 4 ended with two notes linked to C through the half turn: G, at the half turn of C's ring, and F, which has C at the half turn of its own ring. This part gives those links a name, and follows them from note to note.

1. Partners

Call the note at the half turn of a note's ring its partner above. On the frequency ring, that's always the note seven keys up: C's partner above is G, and G's is D.

Turn it round, and every note also has a partner below: the note that has it at the half turn of its own ring. C's partner below is F. F is five keys above C, which leaves seven keys from F up to the next C. That's Part 2's split into seven keys and five once more.

Partners are linked through their harmonics. The half turn is where harmonic 3 lands, and harmonic 3 is the first harmonic that isn't another octave of the note itself (Part 3). So a note's partner above is where its own harmonic 3 lands, beside G for C. Its partner below is the note whose harmonic 3 lands beside it: F's lands beside C.

Musicians: a note's partner above is its perfect fifth, and its partner below is the fifth below it, which is the perfect fourth above it. 3:2 is the simplest ratio after the octave's 2:1.

2. Follow the partners

The ring below is C's ring, spaced by hertz. Press Follow the partner to hop from C to its partner above. Each hop is drawn as a line from a note to its partner.

The first hop goes to G, at the half turn of C's ring. Press again, and the chain hops to G's partner, D, seven keys above G. Then on to A, E, B and F♯. On C's ring the hops don't look alike, because each one is the half turn of a different note's ring, but every hop is the same seven keys up.

Keep going, or press All twelve. After twelve hops the chain is back at C, and on the way it has landed on every one of the twelve notes exactly once: C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯, A♭, E♭, B♭, F, and C again.

Why every note, and why twelve hops? The chain gets back to C only when its hops add up to a whole number of octaves. Seven keys at a time, that first happens after twelve hops: 84 keys, exactly seven octaves. And no note can come up twice before then, because the hops between two visits to the same note would also have to add up to whole octaves.

Switch to Down the chain to follow the partners below instead: C, F, B♭, E♭ and on round, the same chain backwards. So the partners line up all twelve notes in a single chain, with each note between its two partners: … B♭, F, C, G, D …

Musicians: the chain of partners is the circle of fifths. Here it isn't counted out in semitones: it comes from the half turn of the frequency ring, which is where harmonic 3 lands.

3. Evenly spaced keys break the chain

Now drag Spacing to keys. Spaced like the keys, the note at the half turn of every ring is the one six keys up: F♯, for C. Follow that, and the chain goes C, F♯, C, F♯: two notes, back and forth, and the other ten never come up.

Drag Spacing slowly back towards hertz and watch. Just under halfway, the note nearest the half turn changes from six keys up to seven, and the chain jumps from two notes to all twelve.

Evenly spaced keys don't only hide the chain. They put across from C a note that shares no low harmonic with it. Clear the chain, choose Up the chain, and press Note and partner together at each end of Spacing to hear C with each note. F♯'s closest low match with C is its harmonic 5 against C's harmonic 7, 18.6 Hz apart, where G's harmonic 2 and C's harmonic 3 are 0.89 Hz apart.

Musicians: spaced like the keys, the half turn is the tritone, and a tritone above a tritone is back where you started.

4. Four hops to the quarter turn

Clear the chain and follow it four hops up from C: G, D, A, E. E is the note beside C's quarter turn. That isn't luck. Four hops are 4 × 7 = 28 keys, which is two octaves and four keys, and the note four keys above any note sits beside the quarter turn of its ring. So on a piano, a note's quarter-turn note is always four partners along the chain from it.

The two routes to E come from different harmonics, though. The quarter turn is where C's harmonic 5 lands, while four hops take harmonic 3 four times over. On a piano, they arrive at the same key. With hops of exactly ×1½, four hops from middle C land 4.1 Hz above an exact ×1¼. Part 7 comes back to that.

Musicians: four fifths up from C, through G, D and A, is E, a major third two octaves higher. Four pure fifths make the Pythagorean third, 81/64, which is a syntonic comma, about 22 cents, wider than the pure third, 5/4. Equal temperament gives both the same key.

What comes next

Partners line all twelve notes up in one chain, and C sits between its two: F below and G above. A note's quarter-turn note is four partners along the chain from it.

Part 6 puts C, F and G each at the top of its own ring, and looks at which notes sit at their half turns and quarter turns.
