Frequencies of C major, Part 6: Eight out of Twelve

November 2026

Part 6 of 7 in Frequencies of C major.

Previous: Part 5: Following the Partners | Next: Part 7: Pythagoras and Fixed Keys

C major uses seven of the twelve notes: C, D, E, F, G, A and B, the white keys. Played from C up to the next C, that's eight notes, which is where this part's title comes from. Counting each note once, it's seven. Both counts are fair. This part counts seven when it means different notes, and eight when it means the scale played up to the next C.

The question is why those notes. This part builds them from what Parts 1 to 5 laid down: the frequency ring, its half and quarter turns, and the partners.

1. Each note's triangle

Part 3 found where a note's lowest harmonics land on its ring. Harmonics 1, 2 and 4 land at the top, harmonics 3 and 6 at the half turn, and harmonic 5 at the quarter turn. Harmonic 7, at three quarters of a turn, is the first to land between keys. So the first six harmonics point to three keys: the note itself, the key beside its quarter turn, and the key beside its half turn. For C, those are C, E and G.

Joined up on the ring, the three make a triangle. Call it C's triangle. Every ring has the same places (Part 4), so every note has a triangle of the same shape on its own ring: the note, the note four keys up beside its quarter turn, and its partner, seven keys up beside its half turn.

The picture below starts with C's triangle on C's ring. The purple marks are the quarter turn and the half turn of whichever ring you're looking from.

Musicians: a note's triangle is its major triad: harmonics 4, 5 and 6, in the ratio 4:5:6.

2. C and its two partners

Part 5 gave C two partners: F below and G above. Press F's (partner below) and G's (partner above) to add their triangles. Then Look from F, and from G. On its own ring, each partner's triangle has the same shape as C's on C's ring.

F's triangle is F, A and C. G's is G, B and D. With C's, the three triangles hold C, D, E, F, G, A and B: the seven notes of C major, with nothing missing and nothing extra. Press Play the notes in order to hear them as a scale.

So this is where C major's notes come from: C, its two partners, and the notes beside the quarter turn and half turn of their three rings.

Two of them are the notes Part 3 said could never be harmonics of C. F is C's partner below, and A sits beside the quarter turn of F's ring. They come in through F.

There's a choice in this, and it's worth saying plainly. The triangles stop at C's partners, one hop each way along the chain. Go one hop further, to D and B♭, and their triangles bring in F♯ and B♭: nine notes. Seven comes from taking home and its two partners, and no further.

Musicians: F, C and G are the subdominant, tonic and dominant, and their three major triads, IV, I and V, hold every note of the major scale.

3. Seven links in a row

On Part 5's chain, the seven notes are seven links in a row: F, C, G, D, A, E, B. That follows from the triangles. Each triangle is a note, its partner one hop along the chain, and its quarter-turn note four hops along (Part 5). The three notes at the tops are F, C and G, three links in a row. Their partners are C, G and D, one link further on. Their quarter-turn notes are A, E and B, four links further on. Together they fill the seven links from F to B, with no gaps.

Musicians: the white keys are seven consecutive fifths, from F to B. Here that isn't the starting point: it comes out of the three major triads, because a major third is four fifths along.

4. The loose ends

Press Loose ends. At each end of the row the chain is cut: B's partner above is F♯, F's partner below is B♭, and neither is among the seven. F and B are the loose ends.

They're also the only two of the seven that are six keys apart. On evenly spaced keys, six keys is the half turn, so B and F would look like partners. On the frequency ring they aren't: on B's ring, F sits at 149.1°, 30.9° short of the half turn, where F♯ sits.

B is also the one note of the seven whose own triangle doesn't fit. Look from B: E♭ sits beside its quarter turn and F♯ beside its half turn, and neither is among the seven. The nearest the seven offer is B, D and F, with D at 68.1° and F at 149.1°: three keys up instead of four, and six instead of seven. That triangle is lopsided.

Musicians: F to B is the tritone, the only one in C major, and B, D, F is the diminished triad on the seventh degree. Counted from B, the E♭ here is spelled D♯.

5. Evenly spaced keys

Now drag Spacing to keys, with all three triangles on. Spaced like the keys, the note nearest the half turn is six keys up and the note nearest the quarter turn three keys up, so the same rules pick different notes. C's partners, above and below, are both F♯. The triangles are C, E♭, F♯ and F♯, A, C, and together they hold four notes, C, E♭, F♯ and A, each three keys from the next. On evenly spaced keys, the rules can't find C major.

In between, the two turns switch at different points. The half turn moves from six keys to seven just under halfway along Spacing, and the quarter turn from three keys to four at about 62%. Between those points, the rules pick out C, D, E♭, F, G, A♭ and B♭. That in-between ring isn't either picture. It's a blend of the two, and that set appears only in the stretch where one turn has switched and the other hasn't.

Musicians: spaced like the keys, the rules give a diminished seventh chord. The in-between set is C natural minor: three minor triads, built the same way but with the quarter-turn note three keys up.

What comes next

C major is C, its two partners, and the notes beside the quarter and half turns of their three rings. That account leans on the piano's fixed keys. On a piano, a partner is always exactly seven keys up, and four hops along the chain always land exactly on the quarter-turn note. With exact ratios, four hops of ×1½ miss an exact ×1¼ by 4.1 Hz at middle C, and twelve hops don't quite bring the chain back to C.

Part 7 measures those misses, and shows how fixed keys share them out.
