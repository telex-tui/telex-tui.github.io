The Frequency Ring, Part 5: Chords as Shapes

November 2026

Part 5 of 7 in The Frequency Ring.

Previous: Part 4: Each Note's Ring | Next: Part 6: Pythagoras and the Maker's Problem

So far we've mostly looked at pairs of notes: C and G, C and E. Play three notes together and you have a chord. On the frequency ring, three notes join up into a triangle.

This part uses a tuning we haven't met properly yet: pure, for C. Every note is set to an exact whole-number fraction of C: G is exactly 3/2, E exactly 5/4, F exactly 4/3, and so on. These are the spots on the grids from Part 3. Part 6 explains why pianos aren't tuned this way. For now, it's the clearest way to see the shapes.

1. Find the tidy triangles

Tap three notes to make a chord. You'll hear it, and its triangle appears. The first note you tap is the bottom of the chord.

Most triangles come out lopsided. But try F, A, C. Then C, E, G. Then G, B, D. Before reading on, look at what shapes they make.

F, A, C is a perfect equilateral triangle: every corner is 60°. C, E, G is a right-angled triangle with two equal sides. G, B, D is symmetric: its corners at D and B match exactly, mirror images either side of the line from C straight down.

These three chords are among the most common in music, and on C's ring they draw three of the tidiest shapes the ring allows.

Musicians: these are the IV, I and V chords of C major, the three primary triads. On the frequency ring, the key's three most important chords draw some of its most symmetric shapes.

2. Why those shapes?

Turn on the grids from Part 3. F, A and C sit exactly on the thirds, a third of a turn apart, so they can only make an equilateral triangle. C, E and G sit on the quarters. G, B and D sit on the eighths, placed symmetrically either side of the bottom.

The right angle in C, E, G comes for free. C and G are exactly opposite each other, so the line between them runs straight through the middle of the ring. Any triangle drawn on a circle with one side through the middle has a right angle at the third corner. It's a 2,500-year-old result from geometry, credited to Thales. Try C, E♭, G, or C, A♭, G: still right-angled, but no longer with two equal sides.

3. The same chord on its own ring

Tap F, A, C again, then press Root = chord's first note. The ring re-stretches around F, as in Part 4, and the equilateral triangle turns into the right-angled one. Do the same with G, B, D and you get the right-angled one again.

So on its own ring, every one of these chords has the same shape. They're all the same chord, built on different notes. The different shapes on C's ring show something else: how each chord sits in relation to C.

4. Chords as whole numbers

The green badge writes each chord as whole numbers. C, E, G is 4:5:6: for every 4 vibrations of C, E makes 5 and G makes 6. That's Part 3's harmonics 4, 5 and 6, sitting together in one chord. F, A, C and G, B, D are 4:5:6 too.

The order you tap in matters to the badge, even though it doesn't change the triangle. Tap E, G, C: E is now at the bottom and C goes on top. The ring folds octaves together, so the triangle is unchanged, but the badge becomes 5:6:8, because that's how the stacked chord really vibrates.

Other chords need bigger numbers. A, C, E is 10:12:15, and so is C, E♭, G. Turn on the fifths grid to see E♭ land on it. B, D, F needs 45:54:64, and its triangle is the most lopsided of all. Big numbers, lopsided shape, tense sound.

Musicians: 5:6:8 is the first inversion of the major triad. A, C, E and C, E♭, G are minor triads (10:12:15), and B, D, F is the diminished triad in its pure form.

5. The piano bends them

Switch to Piano. Every note moves slightly, and the perfect shapes bend. C, E, G's right angle becomes 90.3°, and its two equal corners become 46.8° and 42.9°. F, A, C's equal corners spread to between 57° and 63°.

That bending is exactly how far the piano's notes sit from the grids. The shapes lean most where E, A and B are involved, because those are the piano's notes furthest from their pure spots.

6. A crack in pure tuning

Back on Pure, for C, set the ring's root to D and tap D, F, A. On its own ring this ought to be as tidy as A, C, E, which is 10:12:15. Instead it's lopsided, and the badge reads 27:32:40.

Tuning every note pure for C has made this chord impure. Part 6 shows this isn't bad luck. It's unavoidable, and it's the problem every instrument maker has to solve.

What comes next

On the frequency ring, chords become triangles. Tuned pure for C, the chords built on F, C and G draw the tidiest shapes, because their notes land on the thirds, quarters and eighths. On its own ring, every major chord draws the same right-angled shape, written as 4:5:6.

But pure tuning for one key breaks chords in others. Part 6 goes back to Pythagoras and finds out why the circle of pure intervals refuses to close.
