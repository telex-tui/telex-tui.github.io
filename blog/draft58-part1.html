Frequencies of C major, Part 1: Keys and Hertz

November 2026

Part 1 of 7 in Frequencies of C major.

Previous: none | Next: Part 2: One Octave as a Ring

This series looks at the notes of C major through their frequencies: how fast each one vibrates. Every picture in it is something you can drag or tap. Nothing is placed by hand: every dot sits where a rule puts it, and the rule is printed underneath.

Part 1 is about the keyboard itself, and what its evenly spaced keys hide.

1. Notes are vibrations

Every note on a piano is a string vibrating back and forth. How high the note sounds depends on how many times the string vibrates each second. That count is the note's frequency, measured in hertz (Hz).

A full-size piano has eight keys called C. Here are their frequencies:

KeyFrequency
C132.7 Hz
C265.4 Hz
C3130.8 Hz
C4261.6 Hz
C5523.3 Hz
C61,046.5 Hz
C72,093.0 Hz
C84,186.0 Hz

Each C vibrates exactly twice as fast as the one below it.

Musicians: these figures assume standard tuning, with the A above middle C at 440 Hz. C4 is middle C, and each C to the next is an octave, a 2:1 ratio of frequencies.

2. Every key multiplies

The twelve keys from one C to the next share that doubling out evenly. Each key vibrates about 1.0595 times as fast as the key below it, and twelve of those multiplications make exactly ×2.

So the keys are evenly spaced, but what each one does to the frequency is multiply. To see what that looks like in hertz, put the keyboard above a frequency axis: a straight line where every hertz takes up the same width. A thread joins each key to its frequency.

The axis below starts out spaced like the keys, so every thread runs straight down. Drag Spacing from keys to hertz and watch the threads lean.

With key spacing, every octave gets a seventh of the width. With hertz spacing, the top octave, C7 to C8, takes half the axis on its own, and the bottom octave gets less than 1%. Each octave covers twice as many hertz as the one below it, so on a frequency axis the low notes crowd into the left-hand corner. Highlight each octave in turn to see its share change as you drag.

Musicians: the keyboard is a logarithmic scale of frequency: equal distances are equal ratios. That's roughly how we hear pitch.

3. Equal keys, growing leaps

Now zoom in to one octave, from middle C to the C above. Draw each key as a bar as tall as its frequency.

A row of evenly spaced keys looks like a flight of stairs, every stair the same height. If the keys really were equal leaps in hertz, each bar would rise by the same amount: 21.8 Hz, a twelfth of the octave. The picture below starts there. Drag Leaps from equal to the real keys.

The ends don't move, because both pictures run from C to the next C. But the middle sinks, and the rises change. With the real keys, each rise is about 6% bigger than the last. The first, from C to C♯, is 15.6 Hz. The last, from B to C, is 29.4 Hz, nearly twice as much. Tap any bar to read its rise.

Now watch the dashed line. It marks halfway from C to the next C in hertz: 392.4 Hz, which is ×1½ of middle C. With equal leaps, the sixth key, F♯, sits exactly on it. With the real keys, F♯ drops 22.4 Hz below it, and the line is reached at the seventh key, G, to within half a hertz. That one key's difference is what Part 2 is about.

Change the octave and the numbers change, but the shape doesn't, because every octave is the same twelve multiplications.

Musicians: ×1.0595 is the twelfth root of 2, the equal-tempered semitone. The halfway line is the 3:2 ratio of the perfect fifth, G. F♯, six keys up, is the tritone.

What comes next

Evenly spaced keys are equal multiplications of frequency, and in hertz each one is a bigger leap than the last. The stairs picture, where every leap is the same, puts halfway at F♯. The real keys put it at G.

Part 2 bends one octave of the frequency axis into a ring. Halfway round that ring is ×1½, and it lands on G.
